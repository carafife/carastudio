CaraStudio User Guide

A bodybuilt fork of RawStudio 💪
A simple raw photo developer, designed to take you from beginner toward more advanced tools.

Welcome

CaraStudio is a raw photo developer (the unprocessed files from your camera) that also handles JPEG and TIFF. It lets you adjust exposure, colour, contrast, apply creative effects, then export your images.

The guiding idea: offer a few well-chosen, powerful tools without overwhelming the beginner. Editing is non-destructive — your original file is never altered; all your settings are stored separately and re-applied when you reopen the photo.

Tip: hover over most sliders and buttons to see a help bubble describing their role. Clicking a slider's name resets it to its default value.

Import & browse

Open a folder

In the tool panel (on the right), open the Open tab: the Directory section provides a folder selector to browse your disk. Click a folder: its photos appear in the film strip at the top. CaraStudio remembers the last folder you opened.

Non-RAW photos (JPEG, TIFF, PNG)

By default, 8-bit images (JPEG, TIFF, PNG) are shown alongside RAW files. If you only see RAW files, enable Photo → Load non-RAW images.

Fujifilm X-Trans: the .RAF raw files from Fujifilm cameras with an X-Trans sensor are supported (dedicated demosaicing). They open just like any other raw file.

The film strip and the navigator

  • The film strip (top) shows the folder's thumbnails. Click one to edit it.
  • The navigator (a small overview) helps you find your way when zoomed into the image.
Good to know: thumbnails of already-edited photos regenerate with your effects. Depending on the folder size, this may take a moment in the background.

Clear the thumbnail cache

To speed up display, CaraStudio keeps a thumbnail and a metadata cache for each image (in a hidden .rawstudio folder next to your photos). If an anomaly gets stuck there — a tilted thumbnail, a wrong size, an unexpected colour — use File → Clear thumbnail cache: CaraStudio deletes these thumbnails and metadata for the current folder, then regenerates them cleanly.

Your edits are preserved: only the thumbnail and the metadata cache are deleted. Your editing settings (exposure, curves, crop…) are left untouched.

Sorting photos

Before developing, sort your shots. CaraStudio uses a priority system shown in the left column of the film strip:

KeyEffect
1 2 3Assigns a priority (a star-like rating) to the selected photo.
DMarks the photo as discarded (Deleted).
URemoves the priority (Unsorted).

The priority tabs (* 1 2 3 U D) on the left filter the view: click * to see everything, or a number to show only those photos. On startup, CaraStudio shows "All photos".

Tip: a photo blended with Enfuse automatically gets priority 1, so you can find it easily.

The workspace

The top bar

Below the menus, a button bar gives access to display functions:

  • Film strip / Tool panel: show or hide these areas (Ctrl+I / Ctrl+T).
  • Enfuse (sun icon): blends the selected photos (see Exposure blending).
  • Fullscreen (F11), zoom buttons (see below), Crop, Split views.
  • Thumbnail info: shows or hides the text (filename) under the thumbnails.
  • Non-RAW: shows or hides non-RAW images (JPEG, TIFF, PNG) alongside RAW files in the film strip — the folder reloads on each click.
  • Pipeline (on the right): opens the legend of the 5 processing stages (see The pipeline).
  • The FR / EN button (on the right) changes the interface language (see Language).
Tip: Ctrl+R reloads and refreshes the current folder's display at any time (handy after adding or changing files in the folder).

Zoom: the whole picture, or the detail

Four buttons, in order: −, 1, whole photo, +.

ButtonWhat it does
1Shows the image at 100% — one photo pixel per screen pixel. It is the only scale at which sharpness and noise can be judged.
Whole photo (four corners)Fits the entire image back into the window. It is the counterpart of the 100% button: you go back and forth between the two constantly.
− and +Gradual zoom, in steps, around the current level.

The mouse wheel over the image also zooms, centred on the pointer. When the image is larger than the window, hold the middle button and move the mouse to pan around it.

Good to know: zoom is capped at 200%. Beyond that the preview would have to recompute the whole image at the requested scale, which would get very slow without showing anything more: at 100% you already see every pixel in the file.

Compare before/after: the A / B / C snapshots

At the top of the tool panel, three buttons A, B, C are independent snapshots of your settings. Adjust the photo in A, switch to B to try another version: you can compare two developments of the same image. The Split views button (Ctrl+D) shows two versions side by side.

Lights Out

F12 toggles "Lights Out" mode: the interface fades to a black background so you can judge the image without distraction.

The processing pipeline

A pipeline is the processing chain your photo travels through, top to bottom, before it appears on screen: each stage receives the result of the previous one, transforms it, and hands over to the next. It is a bit like a darkroom line: you develop the negative before making a print, never the other way round.

The key — and often counter-intuitive — point: in CaraStudio, the order of the modules on screen does NOT match the real order of processing. White balance sits at the very top of the panel, yet lens correction (further down) is actually applied before it. The order that matters is the pipeline below:

The 5 stages in detail

StageWhat it doesModules involved
AGeometryLens correction, rotation, cropping. Processed first, even though it sits lower on screen.Lens, Crop
BRAW developmentThe heart of developing: white balance, exposure, channel mixer, saturation/hue/contrast, then the Value curve.White balance, Basic, Curve (Value tab)
CNoise reductionDenoising (luminance and colour) and sharpening.Basic (Sharpness, Denoise)
DEffectsAll the creative treatments, in a fixed internal order: Argentico → Dehaze → DynaComp → Tone doctor → Color balance → Color scalpel → RGB curves → Black & White → Soft light → Vignette.Effects, Tone, Curve (R/G/B tabs)
EOutputConversion to the display colour space and helper overlays (exposure mask).Display, export

Why the order changes everything

Since each stage works on the result of the previous one, a module placed after another can "undo" or alter its effect. Examples:

  • Denoising (C) smooths the image after development (B): no point pushing sharpness in Basic if an effect (D) will then rework the edges.
  • The Tone (D) tools act after the Value curve (B): they already see a contrasted image, not the raw RAW.
  • The vignette (D) darkens the edges at the very end of the creative chain, on top of everything else.
The letter + colour code. To make this order visible, every module carries a small badge (A, B, C, D, E) in the colour of its stage. The Pipeline button in the top bar recalls the legend at any time. A single block can straddle two stages: the Curve has a Value tab badged B (in development) and R/G/B tabs badged D (among the effects) — visual proof that a tool's place in the chain matters.

BWhite balance

The first module in the Tools panel, white balance corrects a colour cast (too warm/orange, too cold/blue). Three ways to do it:

ToolUse
EyedropperClick the eyedropper icon, then click a neutral area of the image (a grey, a non-blown white): CaraStudio neutralises the colour from that point.
Auto (AWB)Computes an automatic average balance.
CameraUses the balance recorded by the camera at capture time.

You can also adjust Temperature (warm ↔ cool) and Tint (green ↔ magenta) manually.

Exposure mask

The Exposure mask button (in the white balance row) overlays a visual aid on the preview: over-exposed areas (blown white, with no detail) and sometimes under-exposed areas are colour-coded, so you can spot at a glance what is being lost. It is a display overlay (pipeline stage E): it does not alter the photo, it guides you while you set the exposure. Click again to remove it.

RAW vs JPEG: white balance is most flexible on RAW files. On a JPEG (already developed by the camera), adjustments are still possible but more limited.

BCamera color profiles (DCP)

Every camera sensor has its own color "signature": two cameras shooting the same scene do not record exactly the same hues. A DCP profile (Digital Camera Profile, a standard format created by Adobe) describes precisely how to convert the raw colors of one specific camera into accurate colors. Without a dedicated profile, CaraStudio falls back on a generic conversion: correct, but less refined — skin tones, saturated reds or foliage greens may lack nuance.

At the very top of the Tools tab, the profile selector shows the profile used for the current photo. Unfold it to switch profiles or add one.

Installing a profile for your camera

StepAction
1Get a .dcp file matching your camera (see sources below).
2Open a photo from that camera, unfold the profile selector (top of the Tools tab) and pick "Add profile…". Select the .dcp file: it is copied into your personal profile library.
3Select the profile in the list: the photo's rendering changes immediately.
4To apply it automatically to every photo from this camera: menu Photo → Save Camera Default Settings. The bottom bar confirms ("Settings saved as default for…"). From now on, each photo from this camera loads with this profile.
Careful: step 4 saves all the photo's current settings (exposure, saturation, white balance…) as defaults, not just the profile. Preferably do it on a photo with untouched settings, right after picking the profile. A confirmation dialog reminds you and names the camera concerned.

Camera defaults: what they change, and how to undo them

Saved defaults are not a mere shortcut: they become the camera's reference. Two consequences worth knowing:

EffectDetail
New photosEvery photo from this camera now opens with these settings instead of the factory values.
ResetReset returns to these defaults, not to the factory values. That is intended — but if the defaults hold an unwanted setting, no reset will appear to work.

The way out. Open Photo → Save Camera Default Settings again and choose « Clear existing defaults ». The camera returns to factory values and your DCP profile stays attached — you do not lose your calibration work.

The right habit: before saving defaults, click Reset and then pick only the DCP profile. You get a « profile only » default, with no exposure or white balance frozen in.

Where to find DCP profiles

SourceDetails
RawTherapee (installed)If it is installed on your machine, hundreds of profiles are already in /usr/share/rawtherapee/dcpprofiles/ — help yourself!
ART (installed)Same thing in ART's dcpprofiles folder (e.g. /usr/local/share/ART/dcpprofiles/).
RawTherapee (online)The same profiles, freely downloadable: github.com/Beep6581/RawTherapee — rtdata/dcpprofiles folder (click a file, then "Download raw file").
Adobe DNG ConverterFree (Windows/macOS): helpx.adobe.com — Adobe DNG Converter. Its installation ships Adobe .dcp profiles for virtually every camera on the market — retrievable from its CameraProfiles folder.
Make your ownWith a ColorChecker target and the free tool dcamprof (torger.se): the profile will be calibrated on your very camera. For the most dedicated.
Camera too recent, no profile available? Use the profile of a camera with a closely related sensor (often the previous generation in the same line, or a sibling model): the rendering will already be far more refined than the generic conversion. Real-world example: the Nikon Z f profile works very well on a Nikon Z5 II, whose sensor is a close cousin.
Where do your profiles live? Added profiles are copied into your personal folder (~/.rawstudio/profiles/) and the per-camera choice is stored in your configuration — your photos themselves are never modified.

BCBasic adjustments

Still in the Tools tab, the Basic block gathers the essential adjustments. It straddles two pipeline stages: exposure and colours belong to development B, whereas sharpening and denoising are handled later, at stage C.

SettingRole
ExposureBrightens or darkens the whole image.
SaturationColour intensity (0 = black and white).
HueShifts the overall colour.
ContrastThe gap between light and dark tones.
SharpnessAccentuates detail C.
Denoise / Color denoiseReduces digital noise, luminance and colour C.

The Auto and Reset buttons

A row of buttons, below the sliders, automates the common adjustments:

ButtonRole
Auto-exposureAutomatically sets the exposure for a balanced overall brightness. Stable result: re-applying it does not drift.
Auto-levelsStretches the contrast by setting a black point and a white point (gently, to avoid excess). Complementary to auto-exposure, and independent of it.
ResetRestores the Basic block to its default values in a single click.
Tip: auto-exposure and auto-levels are two separate "autos" — one sets brightness, the other contrast. Use either, or both.

Channel mixer

The Red / Green / Blue sliders dose each channel's contribution. Very useful in black and white to lighten or darken specific colours (a blue sky, skin tones…).

Tip: each slider's value is shown on the right. Click a slider's name to reset it; click its value to type one; the scroll wheel fine-tunes.

ALens & curve

Lens correction A

Every lens has small optical flaws: darkened corners (vignetting), coloured fringes along high-contrast edges (chromatic aberration), slightly bent straight lines (distortion). This module corrects them. It is a geometric stage, applied at the very start of the pipeline A.

What it relies on: the lensfun database. CaraStudio builds on lensfun, a free, community-maintained database that catalogues the optical characteristics of thousands of lenses (measured and shared by enthusiasts). The principle: you link your real lens to its lensfun entry; CaraStudio then reads that lens's flaw "signature" and automatically applies the inverse corrections (de-vignetting, distortion and chromatic-aberration correction) — with no manual tweaking. The CA R, CA B and Vign sliders remain for fine manual adjustment if needed.

Assigning a lens. The Edit lens button opens the editor. Click Set lens (or Lens Library for the full list), then pick your lens's maker and model from the lensfun list. Tick Enable this lens to apply the correction, and Enable Defish to straighten a fisheye. The button stays available so you can always re-pick.

Important — the correction is PER LENS, not per photo. Once a lens is assigned, its correction automatically applies to every photo taken with that lens.
  • Modern lenses (electronic): each lens is recognised automatically from EXIF. So within one folder, photos taken with different lenses each get their own lens's correction, automatically.
  • Manual / legacy lenses (no electronics, e.g. Helios-44): CaraStudio can't tell them apart (no EXIF) → it groups them by camera body. The assigned profile then applies to all manual-lens photos from that body.
Tip: if a session mixes several manual lenses, sort them into separate folders (one lens per folder) — you can then apply the right profile to each whole folder.

Curve B D

The curve gives precise control over brightness by ranges. The block is organised into four tabs:

  • Value — the classic tonal curve (brightness). It is part of development B.
  • R, G, B — one curve per red, green, blue channel, to tint the colours (warm cast in the highlights, cool in the shadows…). These curves act among the effects D.

On each curve:

  • Left click: add a point; Shift + left click: delete a point.
  • Raise the curve to brighten, lower it to darken; an "S" shape strengthens contrast.
  • The Reset button flattens the current tab (linear curve).

Preset curves & saving

A menu button offers ready-to-use preset curves (soft, strong contrast…) to start from a base. You can also save your own curve, reload it later or delete it — handy to reuse a look from one photo to the next.

ACropping

The Crop button (or Shift+C) opens the cropping tool. A small floating palette appears over the image:

  • Choose a fixed ratio (1:1, 3:2, 16:9…) or a free crop.
  • A grid (rule of thirds…) helps you compose.
  • Click OK (or Enter) to apply the crop; Cancel crop (or Esc) removes it.

Cropping is non-destructive: you can reopen and change it at any time.

Undo, copy, reset

The Edit menu gathers everything about managing a photo's settings: stepping back, carrying a look over to other photos, or starting fresh.

Stepping back (Ctrl+Z)

Edit → Revert Settings (Ctrl+Z) restores the settings as they were when the photo was opened.

Important — this is not a classic undo. It does not step back action by action, slider by slider, the way a word processor does. There is a single return point: the photo's state when you opened it. Pressing Ctrl+Z repeatedly will not go back any further.

In practice: if you opened the photo, made ten adjustments, then pressed Ctrl+Z, all ten vanish at once. And if the photo was already developed when you opened it, Ctrl+Z returns you to that development — not to the untouched raw.

Starting fresh

Edit → Reset Settings returns the photo to its default values. The Reset button in the Basic module does the same.

If you have saved camera defaults, resetting returns to those defaults rather than the factory values. See Camera defaults: what they change.

Carrying a look to other photos

CommandRole
Copy SettingsStores all the current photo's settings.
Paste SettingsApplies them to the photo — or to every photo selected in the filmstrip.
Copy/paste or a style? Copy/paste carries everything, right now, and does not survive between sessions. A style is named, stored for good, and lets you pick which modules to carry over. For a one-off between two photos, copy/paste is enough; for a look you will reuse, make a style.
The DCP profile is never carried over, neither by copy/paste nor by styles: it belongs to the camera.

Preferences

Edit → Preferences opens the application settings: preview background colour, histogram height, cache location, interface theme, display colour profiles, and quick-export options.

DEffects

The Effects tab gathers the creative treatments. They form stage D of the pipeline and run in a fixed internal order (Argentico → Dehaze → DynaComp → Tone doctor → Color balance → Color scalpel → RGB curves → Black & White → Soft light → Vignette), always after the base development.

Dehaze

Clears mist and atmospheric haze for more clarity and local contrast. The Dehaze slider doses the effect, Saturation compensates the colour.

DynaComp (dynamic range compressor)

A very contrasty scene — a backlit subject, an interior with a sunlit window, woodland with patches of bright sky — often exceeds what a screen or a print can show: either the shadows block up, or the highlights "blow out". The dynamic range compressor narrows that gap by bringing both extremes closer together: it lifts the shadows AND holds back the highlights, so all the information fits into a visible range.

Its strength is that it works locally: it compresses the large areas of brightness while preserving micro-contrast (textures, fine detail), where a plain global curve would flatten the image.

SettingRole
AmountStrength of the compression. At 0, no effect. Raise it to open up the shadows and recover the highlights; too strong, and the result looks "flat" or unreal.
ThresholdBoosts micro-contrast (local detail) to restore "bite" after compression. Lower it for a softer look.
Use with restraint: a gentle setting is often enough. On very sharp transitions (a dark edge against bright sky), a strong setting can create a faint bright halo — reduce the Amount in that case.

Soft light

A "soft light" effect that softens the image and gives a luminous, velvety look.

Artistic vignette

Darkens (or lightens) the edges to draw the eye to the subject. Adjust the strength, the roundness and the feather (smoothness of the gradient).

Black & White

Converts to black and white with a channel mixer (Red, Orange, Yellow, Green, Cyan, Blue, Violet): push a colour to lighten the matching areas. Colour filters and film looks (Panchromatic, Orthochromatic…) are available.

Argentico (film negative)

Simulates the development of a film negative: layer ratios and development exposure, for a film-like rendering.

DTone

The Tone tab offers three advanced tools ported from the ART software.

Tone doctor (tone equalizer)

An equalizer that works by brightness ranges: Blacks, Shadows, Midtones, Highlights, Whites, around an adjustable pivot. Ideal for rebalancing an over-contrasted image or recovering detail in shadows/highlights.

Color balance (3-way wheels)

Three colour wheels — Shadows, Midtones, Highlights — to tint each tonal range (cinema-style colour grading). Each wheel sets the colour and luminance (lift / gamma / gain).

Wheel or sliders, your choice. Under each wheel, three sliders always show the zone's setting:

SliderRole
HueThe angle on the wheel, 0 to 360° (0° = red, 120° = green, 240° = blue). Picks which colour is added.
IntensityThe distance from centre, 0 to 1. Sets how much of that colour is added. At 0 the zone is neutral.
LuminanceBrightens or darkens the tonal range without changing its colour.

Wheel and sliders are two views of the same setting and stay in sync: drag the point on the wheel and the sliders follow; move a slider and the point moves. The wheel is quick and intuitive, the sliders give precision and repeatability from one photo to the next.

Tip: while Intensity is 0 the zone is neutral, so changing Hue has no visible effect — that is expected, there is no colour yet to steer. Raise Intensity first, then look for the hue. Right-click the wheel to return the zone to centre (neutral); click a slider's name to reset it.
Good to know: each wheel mainly acts on its own tonal range, but the three overlap gradually — on a very dark photo the Highlights wheel naturally has little to work with, and likewise Shadows on a very bright one.

Color scalpel (per-hue curves)

Finely adjusts each colour family independently, on three curves (Hue, Saturation, Luminance tabs) drawn over the hue spectrum. Perfect to, for instance, revive a sky without touching skin tones. The curve is smoothed (soft transitions between points), and the adjustment fades on low-saturation colours (whites, greys) to avoid halos.

GestureAction
Left-click + dragMoves the nearest point (raises/lowers the correction for that hue).
Ctrl + left-clickAdds a point where you click.
Ctrl + Shift + left-clickDeletes the nearest point.
Right-clickResets the curve to flat (neutral) — handy to start over if you aimed at the wrong zone.
Halos in the highlights? On a sunset or a very dense sky, pushing a hue's Luminance hard can create a faint bright ring (colours near white are unstable there). CaraStudio already limits the effect near white; if a halo persists, ease off or work the hue/saturation instead.

Correcting straight on the image (hover + wheel)

Setting a curve means knowing where, on the spectrum, the colour you want to change sits. The Hover + wheel on the image button removes that question: you point at the colour on the photo, and the curve follows.

First tick Enable Color scalpel, then the Hover + wheel on the image button. Move the pointer over the photo: a vertical marker appears on the curve, at the hue of the pixel under the cursor. A pointer appears on the image with two concentric circles — the outer one shows the colour before correction, the inner one after — plus a numeric badge.

GestureAction
Wheel over the imageAdjusts the active channel's curve around the hovered hue. One notch = 1% (or 1° on Hue).
Shift + wheelFast adjustment (×4).
Ctrl + wheelFine adjustment (0.1% or 0.1° per notch).
Alt + wheelSwitches channel: Hue → Saturation → Luminance.
Middle buttonPans the image as usual; the pointer returns as soon as you release.

The correction does not apply to the targeted hue alone but spreads gently onto neighbouring hues (roughly 35° either side), so the edit stays natural, with no break between two close colours.

The number freezes but the image keeps changing? That is expected. Each channel has a maximum amplitude — ±180° on Hue, ±100% on Saturation, ±50% on Luminance (that is, luminance multiplied by 1.5). Once the targeted hue is maxed out, the neighbouring hues keep rising: the displayed value stops moving while the effect widens.
A bit slow at 100%? In this mode every mouse move re-reads the colours from the developed image. On a large file shown at 100%, the pointer may lag slightly. Look for the colour in whole photo view, then switch back to 100% to check the result.

Styles (CaraStyles)

Once you've found a look you like on one photo — white balance, curves, colour grading, effects… — you can save it as a style (a preset) and re-apply it in one click to one or many other photos. A great way to give a whole series a consistent look in seconds.

Where the buttons are

In the Tools tab, to the right of the White balance section, a small CaraStyles panel holds three buttons:

ButtonRole
Save (folder)Saves the current photo's settings as a new style.
Apply (arrow)Opens the library and applies a style to the selected photos.
Delete (trash)Removes a style from the library.

The same commands are also in the Edit → Save as style / Apply a style / Delete a style menu.

Saving a style — choosing what to keep

Click Save, give the style a name, then tick the modules to include: Exposure, White balance, Curves, Color balance, Color scalpel, Argentico, Tone doctor, Soft Light, Dehaze, Black & white, Sharpen, Denoise… You can make a "full" style (everything ticked) or a targeted one — for example a colour-grade style that carries only Color balance and Color scalpel, without touching the exposure or crop of the target photos.

Applying a style to one or several photos

  1. First select the thumbnails in the film strip (one or several).
  2. Click Apply and pick a style from the list.
  3. A dialog shows the checkboxes pre-ticked to match what the style contains: you can still refine before confirming (e.g. apply the style without its white balance).
  4. Confirm: the style is applied to all selected photos (and to the open one). Thumbnails update.

The library also lets you rename and delete your styles.

The colour profile stays tied to each photo. A style never carries the DCP profile nor the crop/geometry: those depend on each image's camera and framing. So you can safely apply the same style to a folder that mixes several camera bodies (Nikon, Sony, Panasonic…) — every photo keeps its own colour profile.
Where are styles stored? In ~/.config/carastudio/styles/ (one .carastyle file per style). You can back them up or share them by copying these files.

Info & keywords

Info tab (EXIF)

The Info tab shows the photo's metadata: camera, lens, aperture, shutter speed, ISO, plus an extended list of EXIF fields (exposure program, metering mode, GPS, author…). A search field at the top filters the rows (case- and accent-insensitive).

Keywords (Open tab)

Keyword management lives in the Open tab, under Tag Search (not in the Info tab). There you can add and remove keywords on the selected photo(s), with autocompletion. Keywords make later sorting and searching easier.

Exposure blending (Enfuse)

CaraStudio blends several photos of the same scene shot at different exposures (bracketing) into a single, balanced image — useful for very contrasted scenes (interior + window, backlit landscape).

  • First select the photos to blend in the film strip (at least two).
  • Click the Enfuse button (sun icon) in the top bar — or the Photos → Enfuse menu, or Ctrl+Alt+E.
  • The Extend option widens the range by generating darker/lighter variants.
  • The blended photo is created automatically in the source folder and receives priority 1.
Nothing selected? If you click the Enfuse button without having chosen any photos, a message invites you to select some first — blending makes no sense on a single isolated image.

EExporting

When you are happy with your settings, export the developed image. Your original files are never overwritten: exporting creates new files.

Which version is exported? The current view (snapshot A / B / C)

Key point: CaraStudio always exports the current view, that is, the development of the currently active snapshot (the A, B or C button highlighted at the top of the tool panel). Your settings are not a single frozen state of "the photo": each snapshot is an independent version of the same image.

  • If snapshot B is active, it is version B that gets exported.
  • To export another version, first switch to the snapshot you want (A, B or C), then export.
Remember: you export what you see. The original RAW/JPEG file itself always stays intact — exporting produces a separate new file.

The two modes

  • Quick export: saves the current photo with the current output settings.
  • Batch processing (Batch tab): add several photos to the queue and export them all at once.

Adding photos to the batch (snapshots A / B / C)

Two commands feed the batch queue, depending on whether you want a single version or several:

CommandEffect
Add to Batch Queue
Insert		Adds the current photo (or the photos selected in the film strip) to the queue, with the active snapshot — the view you see on screen.
Add View to Queue…
Ctrl+Insert		Opens a window "Select which settings to add to batch queue" with three checkboxes A, B, C (the current snapshot's box is already ticked). Tick one, several or all three snapshots to queue several versions of the same photo in one go.
Tip: "Add View to Queue" is ideal for exporting, in a single pass, two different developments of the same image — for example a colour version (snapshot A) and a black & white version (snapshot B).

Output settings

The Edit Output Settings button (Batch tab) opens the window that controls how your images are saved:

SettingRole
File TypeOutput format: JPEG (compressed, ideal for sharing), PNG or TIFF (lossless, heavier files).
JPEG QualityFrom 1 to 100%. The higher the value, the better the image but the larger the file. 85–95% is a good compromise (default ~90). Not applicable to PNG/TIFF, which are lossless.
Output DirectoryThe folder where the exported files are written.

Export dimensions

The Export Dimensions frame sets the size of the produced images. Choose a mode:

ModeEffect
Constant ScaleResizes by a percentage of the original: 100% = full size, 50% = half size.
Constant WidthSets a fixed width in pixels; the height follows to keep the proportions.
Constant HeightSets a fixed height in pixels; the width follows.
Maximum SizeThe image fits inside a box (max width × height) without distortion — handy for "no more than 2000 px on a side".
Tip: to share on the web, export as JPEG at 85–90% with a constant width of 1600–2048 px: light and sharp files.

Filename template

The Filename Template names your exports automatically from codes; a live example shows the result. Useful codes:

CodeYields
%fOriginal filename
%2cIncremental counter (here 2 digits)
%dCapture date (YYYY-MM-DD)

Example: %f_%2c produces IMG1234_01, IMG1234_02…

Open in GIMP

The GIMP button (top bar), or the File → Export to GIMP menu, renders the current view with all your settings and opens it in GIMP for pixel editing. CaraStudio detects GIMP automatically (installed by your distribution or as a Flatpak).

GIMP not found? On some installations — the AppImage in particular — CaraStudio may not locate GIMP on its own. On the first click, a window then asks you to point to the GIMP executable (for example /usr/bin/gimp, or a GIMP AppImage). The path is remembered: you only need to do this once.

Appearance & language

Interface language

The FR / EN button on the right of the top bar switches the interface between French and English. The label shows the language you will switch to. CaraStudio will offer to restart to apply the change (your settings are saved before relaunching).

Interface theme

In Edit → Preferences, the Theme selector changes the application's look. The change is immediate — no restart — and your choice is remembered.

ThemeMood
CaraStudioThe original palette, neutral mid-grey. Default choice.
Studio AnthraciteDeep charcoal grey, azure accent. The darkest: the interface steps back and lets the image speak.
Studio ClairLow-contrast mid-grey, for brightly lit rooms.
Studio AmbreSame dark base, golden accent instead of blue.
Why a grey background and not white? The eye judges a colour relative to its surroundings. A neutral, dark background keeps you from misreading hues and exposure — which is why every raw developer is dark. When in doubt, stay on a dark theme for grading.
These themes were contributed by Christian Bouhon, along with the wheel sliders in Color balance. Many thanks to him!

The lens library (Ctrl+L)

It lists every lens found in your photos and whether it is matched to the lensfun database. This is where you attach an unrecognised lens to its entry, which switches on the automatic corrections. The procedure is detailed in Lens correction.

Recent lens not recognised? The lensfun database installed on your system may be old. Update it (your distribution's lensfun package, or the lensfun-update-data command): recent lenses are often already in it, with their measurements.

About & community

CaraStudio is developed by Carafife, a photography and free-software enthusiast. For many years I have run a YouTube channel dedicated to creating tutorials on free (open-source) photo software. I am also one of the founders and an active member of the Artherapee forum. My goal: to help the community advance free software in the world of photography.

My aim with CaraStudio is to offer a bridge between a simple photo application and a more elaborate one: staying approachable for the beginner, while gradually slipping in more advanced tools. That is also why several of its modules are directly inspired by those of the ART software.

A question, an idea, a bug to report, keen to share your photos? Join the community:

  • CaraStudio Discussions — github.com/carafife/carastudio/discussions: help, ideas and sharing.
  • Report a bug / suggest an improvement — github.com/carafife/carastudio/issues.
  • YouTube channel — youtube.com/@carafife: tutorials on free photo software.

And if CaraStudio ever starts to feel too limited, that is actually a good sign: it is precisely designed to prepare you for ART, a more complete open-source software where you will find, in a more accomplished form, most of the tools discovered here. The Artherapee forum will support you through that transition.
